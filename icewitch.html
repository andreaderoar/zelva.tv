The Ice Witch
Creating the Hook

How we designed the characters, built the world, and directed Seedance 2.0 shot by shot — every prompt included.

ToolsMidjourney · ChatGPT · Photoshop · Seedance 2.0 · Premiere
PlatformsDreamina · Higgsfield
Runtime~60 second cold open

The Ice Witch is an origin story. But origin stories have a problem on social media: they start slow, and viewers don't wait. So before a single frame of Aelira's childhood, we open twenty-five winters later — a hooded prisoner dragged through a screaming mob, tied to the stake, and set on fire. The town celebrates. Then the fire dies, the temperature drops, and the woman they tried to burn shows them what a monster really looks like.

Hooks that go straight into action hold viewer retention far better than a linear opening. When the carnage ends and the title card reads "25 Winters Earlier," the audience isn't watching a backstory anymore — they're watching an answer to a question: how did she become this?

This is the complete breakdown of how the hook was made: the exact Midjourney prompts behind every character and the location, why we deliberately avoided Nano Banana Pro, the Dreamina-to-Higgsfield pipeline, and the full Seedance 2.0 prompt for every section of the sequence.

Chapter 01

Character Design in Midjourney

Studio-lit reference sheets, built like a real casting and wardrobe session.

Every character starts as a reference sheet: one frontal close-up for identity, one full-body for wardrobe, generated in a single 16:9 frame against a clean studio backdrop. Bright, even lighting — not because the film looks like that, but because the video model needs clean information. Mood comes later; references are for facts.

Aelira, the Ice Witch

The hook needs her twice: hooded and unknowable during the march, revealed when the fire dies. We generated the base prisoner look in Midjourney, then took her into ChatGPT to swap in our actual main character's face — once with the hood up, once with the hood down — so the woman burned at the stake is unmistakably the same woman from the origin story.

Chapter 02

Why Midjourney — and not Nano Banana Pro

Your references set the ceiling for your film.

Here's the lesson that cost us the most credits to learn: the video model inherits the cinematography of your reference images. Feed Seedance flat, evenly lit references and you get flat, evenly lit video — no prompt can fully rescue it.

Nano Banana Pro is a capable model, but its default look is bright and clean — closer to a 2010 TV series or a commercial. Midjourney, with dialed-in style references, produces genuine film-still cinematography: deep shadows, cool rim light, texture, atmosphere. We spent real time refining our SREF codes to lock that look, and it pays off in every frame of video. Same character, both models, judge for yourself:

The ruleNever write just "cinematic." The look comes from the reference images and from naming the actual craft: the camera, the lens, the depth of field, the movement, the composition. The same applies to locations — a flat location plate will drop your Seedance quality and shift your lighting.

Generated in Midjourney, with small details fixed in ChatGPT.

Chapter 04

The Seedance 2.0 Pipeline

Test cheap, render expensive, edit like a film.

With the assets locked, generation follows a strict routine — built to protect both the credits and the look:

Step 1

Test at 720p — Dreamina

Why pay for 1080 when you aren't sure about the results? Every prompt gets dialed in against cheap 720p tests first.

Step 2

Iterate the prompt

Each test run adjusts blocking, physics constraints and timing until the shot behaves. Multiple runs per section.

Step 3

Final — Higgsfield 1080p

Higgsfield's 1080p with non-compression mode is a world of difference above Dreamina's 1080 — that's where finals render.

Step 4

Edit — Premiere

Best takes cut together, sound design fixed, a little music. The finale alone is several generations edited into one.

Every prompt is built with a custom Claude Skill we wrote for live-action work. As a cinematographer, I want that smooth handheld feeling where the camera is alive — moving with the action, usually shooting from the darker side. I don't own a set of anamorphic Cooke lenses in real life, but Seedance understands the language beautifully. Each scene is timed and composed to fit inside Seedance's 15-second clip limit, and every prompt names the emotion, blocking and choreography of the characters — that's what makes them feel alive instead of posed.

Chapter 05

The Hook, Prompt by Prompt

Five Seedance 2.0 sections, in story order — with the frames they produced.

Each prompt follows the same architecture: numbered reference images, art style, environment, context, blocking, a shot list with purpose / movement / frame / duration, then lighting, physics and audio. Read the blocking sections closely — every "never" and "only" in there is a scar from a failed generation.

Section 01The March6 shots · 15s

The executioner drags Aelira by a rope through the mob, ties her to the post, delivers the sentence, and lights the pyre. The blocking spells out the single-file geometry — who leads, where the rope runs, what she never does — because a video model will happily invent its own.

Environment as seen in <<<image_5>>>. Blue hour dusk, trampled snow and scorched ground, torch flames flickering, faint falling snow. A huge crowd of at least fifty villagers packs the execution ground, filling the background and edges of every shot even though the framing stays close.

CONTEXT
A prisoner is marched to the stake. The executioner leads, she follows on his rope.

BLOCKING
This is a single file prisoner march. Both characters walk in the SAME direction the entire time, from frame left toward frame right, toward the pyre which is always off frame right. The executioner walks in front. Aelira walks two meters behind him, following in his footsteps, facing the same way he faces. They are connected by one rope: the executioner grips one end in his right fist, the rope runs backward through the air behind him and its other end is knotted around Aelira's wrists, which are tied together in front of her body. She walks only because the rope pulls her forward, her arms drawn slightly ahead of her by the tension. He never faces her while marching, his back is to her. She never overtakes him, never walks beside him, never walks the opposite way, never holds the rope. When he yanks the rope she lurches a quick step forward but never falls. The crowd of at least fifty men lines both sides of their path, faces turned toward her. Only at the scaffold does he turn around to face her and tie her to the blackened post, where she stands facing camera with the crowd massed below.

Section 05The Freeze & The Smiletwo prompts · edited together

The finale. Honest production note: this one took multiple runs of two prompt variants, and the final sequence is the best pieces edited together in Premiere. The physics writing does the heavy lifting — the freeze must be gradual, spreading back-to-front over the running bodies, never instant and never from the ground up, because "instant" is exactly what the model defaults to. It ends on the shot the entire hook was built for: the smile.

What We'd Tell You

  1. Your references set the ceiling. Midjourney's cinematography survives into the video — a flat reference never recovers, no matter the prompt.
  2. One face per reference sheet. Remove the head from the full-body wardrobe shot so the model has a single source of identity.
  3. Never write "cinematic." Name the camera, the lens, the depth of field, the movement, the composition — the actual craft.
  4. Write emotion, blocking and choreography for every character in every shot. That's the difference between performers and mannequins.
  5. Every "never" in a blocking section is a scar. Write defensive constraints for whatever the model keeps breaking — planted feet, whole bodies, clear lenses.
  6. Test at 720p, render at the best 1080 you can get, and edit like a film: multiple takes, best pieces, real sound design.

The prompt is everything — and we keep learning with every film. If you build something with these, we'd genuinely love to see it.
